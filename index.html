THE PROBLEM

Physical danger is measurable.
Warning access is harder to see.

✳ THE PROBLEM 01

A forecast is not
a warning.

In spring 2024, floodwater reached streets and homes across Kazakhstan. But the crucial policy question is not only whether authorities could predict rising rivers. It is whether the right information reached residents in time, and whether they had a realistic way to respond.

Petropavl sits along the Esil (Ishim) River in North Kazakhstan, an area with a long history of seasonal flooding. On 16 April 2024, the president's office reported that the flood wave was passing through several districts and the city. The regional report recorded 10,345 people evacuated and more than 2,000 flooded homes on that date. These were regional figures, not totals for Petropavl city alone. [01]

UNDP's November 2026 account describes more than 5,900 evacuated residents and over 2,100 affected homes around Petropavl during the 2024 event. The source uses a different geographic framing and was published later; those values should not be merged with the April regional snapshot as though they measured identical populations. [09]

Flood exposure is not the same as warning vulnerability. A household may be physically exposed but receive a clear alert; another may receive a message but have no transport, accessible shelter, or confidence in the information. An early-warning policy must consider access, understanding, trust, and capacity to act, not just forecast accuracy.

We do not yet have representative Petropavl household data proving that older residents, rural households, or low-income residents received fewer warnings in 2024. These are possible mechanisms to investigate, not findings we may assume. Understanding the differences these explanations is central to the social-science contribution of this project.

The main problem
RESEARCH QUESTION

Can AI-assisted, community-based warning coordination improve timely access to actionable flood information in North Kazakhstan (Petropavl) without reproducing inequalities in who gets protected?

Case: Petropavl and flood-prone settlements nearby. Unit of analysis for a proposed pilot: affected communities and their warning channels, rather than personal risk scores.

What makes this a social-science problem?

01

Institutional capacity

Hydrometeorological agencies, local authorities, and emergency services must coordinate. A prediction can fail to become a usable message when responsibilities are fragmented.

02

Communication inequality

Different channels reach different audiences. Digital alerts, interpersonal networks, and public announcements have unequal coverage and accessibility.

03

Trust & agency

People interpret a warning through earlier experiences and practical constraints. Receiving an alert does not mean one can leave immediately.

SCOPE / 2024–2026

What exactly is being studied?

The project examines flood-warning communication in Petropavl and nearby flood-prone settlements. The 2024 floods establish the scale of the hazard, while later developments show how Kazakhstan's forecasting infrastructure has evolved. The proposed pilot compares community warning pathways, recognizing that local transport, communication coverage, and institutional capacity may differ.

WHAT THIS TELLS US

The scale of an emergency.

The five snapshots illustrate the evolving number of people accounted for in national rescue and evacuation operations. They provide credible evidence of the scale of the response. Taken with regional information, they justify examining flood preparedness and emergency communication in North Kazakhstan.

WHAT IT CANNOT TELL US

Who got the warning.

None of these reports identifies the share of households who received an alert, how early it arrived, which channel was used, whether it was understood, or whether residents had a practical opportunity to act. These outcomes would require additional data, potentially including carefully designed household interviews.

MEASUREMENT PLAN

What data would make the missing part visible?

We would need to connect three different types of evidence without treating them as equivalent. First, hydrological records describe expected and observed river conditions; the quality of the forecast can be judged through lead time and errors. Second, delivery records might reveal how often official messages were dispatched through each channel. These records still would not prove that a person saw or understood a warning. Third, a voluntary household survey, designed with local stakeholders, could ask when people first heard about risk, which source they trusted, what information was unclear, and what practical barriers constrained action.

To assess unequal reach, the survey must document how households were sampled and who could not be contacted. The most disconnected residents may also be hardest to survey, creating nonresponse bias. Comparing responses across neighborhoods would require cautious interpretation, clear denominators, and uncertainty ranges. The aim is not to manufacture a ranking of “vulnerable” people, but to identify communication barriers that responsible institutions could remedy. Until these data exist, our policy recommendation is explicitly conditional.

STATE OF RESEARCH

Research doesn't offer
one simple answer.

✳ CURRENT STATE OF RESEARCH 03

Technology predicts.
Society responds.

The academic literature suggests three distinct problems: estimating physical flood danger, translating warnings into usable messages, and building the institutions and relationships that make action possible. Treating them as the same problem produces incomplete policy.

THEME A 01 — 02

Better forecasts do not settle the communication question.

Nearing and colleagues (2024) show that AI can forecast extreme riverine events in basins without dense gauge networks at useful lead times. That is a technical advance: the paper evaluates predictive reliability, not the proportion of people who understood or acted on alerts. Medeu and Yeltay (2024), by contrast, examine historical flooding in the Esil basin, emphasizing local hydrology and human influence. Together they suggest that transferable modeling must still be interpreted through the specific river system and its institutions. [10] [11]

THEME B 03 — 05

A warning's design and delivery matter.

Kuller, Schoenholzer and Lienert (2021) synthesize 21 elements in warnings' content, format, and channels, emphasizing context rather than a universal message template. Shrestha and colleagues (2021) use qualitative evidence from Nepal to show how socio-cultural conditions and coordination affect the last mile. Hasan and Islam (2024) compare two river communities in Bangladesh, finding uneven warning effectiveness in a small, locally bounded sample. These studies show why messaging cannot be evaluated solely by counting broadcasts. Their specific findings, however, are not automatically transferable to Petropavl. [12] [13] [14]

THEME C 06 — 07

Equity requires participation and institutional design.

Painter and colleagues (2025) review the technological and social foundations of flood early-warning systems. They argue for community-informed design, continuity, transparency, and local capacity. Hamidi and colleagues (2026) develop a framework of communication-related vulnerability and apply it to a household survey in Pakistan, where access, trust, message design, and information integrity jointly shape response. The Pakistan study supplies a useful mechanism, but its 210 respondents cannot establish the prevalence of the same barriers in Kazakhstan. [15] [16]

A CRITICAL READING

Where these approaches pull in different directions

The studies use different measures of success. Forecasting research prioritizes accuracy and lead time, while communication research examines understanding, trust, and action. Reviews provide broader perspectives, whereas small qualitative studies reveal local mechanisms without establishing population-wide effects. The remaining research gap is a locally evaluated comparison of AI-assisted and human-led flood-warning coordination in Petropavl. [11] [12]

Methods also differ. Reviews integrate findings across many settings but may combine incomparable warning systems. Small qualitative studies are rich in accounts of institutions and everyday constraints but are not representative of all communities. Hydrological retrospective studies describe historical river behavior but rarely measure warning interpretation. The defensible gap is therefore not “nobody has studied flood warnings”; it is the absence, in the evidence assembled here, of a locally evaluated comparison between AI-assisted and simpler human-led outreach for Petropavl's warning pathways. [10] [13] [15]

SYNTHESIS / OUR GAP

What is still missing?

Local hydrological knowledge and international AI forecasts are improving, but this evidence does not yet establish the distribution of actionable warning access in Petropavl. Our proposed contribution is a testable coordination model: use AI only where it adds value to a human-led system, then evaluate whether that system reaches households who would otherwise be overlooked.

AI would assist with community-level triage and preparation of standardized bilingual messages. It would not autonomously issue evacuation orders, generate unverified flood depths, rank individuals by personal vulnerability, or replace official emergency services.

PILOT DESIGN / HOW TO TEST THE CLAIM

Build a service that can be evaluated, not an unaccountable black box.

The first step should be an inventory of official data permissions, coverage and communication responsibilities. The second is a non-AI reference workflow: authorities use existing flood thresholds, carefully written bilingual messages and community outreach teams to coordinate warnings. The proposed AI layer would then be tested against this practical benchmark, not against an artificially weak baseline that sends only a single phone notification.

Where permitted, the model could estimate which areas warrant additional outreach based on verified flood exposure, past delivery gaps and infrastructure-level indicators. It would not need names, phone numbers or sensitive household profiles to recommend that a particular neighborhood receive extra human attention. Any language-generation component should be constrained to approved terminology and templates; staff must check all factual, geographic and timing information before release. Uncertain predictions should be disclosed rather than translated into false precision.

Evaluation should compare timely receipt, message understanding, appropriate action and operational cost, disaggregated only where ethical and statistically meaningful. Every group must continue to receive the standard legally required emergency warnings. The pilot could test added services or phased rollout without withholding basic safety information. Pre-specified stopping rules should address repeated incorrect alerts, weak coverage for specific communities, privacy incidents and the absence of demonstrable improvement over the non-AI system.

✳ INTERNATIONAL EVIDENCE CASE STUDIES

Real systems.
Different outcomes.

The closest precedents do not all test the same thing. A responsible transfer to Kazakhstan must distinguish forecasting accuracy, emergency operations, and household preparedness.

01 / AI FLOOD FORECASTINGGLOBAL · GOOGLE

Google Flood Hub

Google's AI river forecasting is operational in numerous countries and was evaluated in a 2024 Nature study. The study found useful predictive reliability for extreme floods even in ungauged basins. This is robust evidence for a forecasting capability; it does not establish what fraction of households ultimately acted on a warning. [11] [17]

What transfers: model integration and lead-time potentialWhat does not: measured behavior in Petropavl
02 / LIVE FLOOD INTELLIGENCEAUSTRALIA · FLOODMAPP

FloodMapp

FloodMapp provides continuously updated inundation mapping and forecast products to emergency and infrastructure operators. Its publicly available case studies describe use by agencies in Queensland and elsewhere. This documents deployment, but many descriptions come from the vendor, so independent evaluation of effectiveness is a limitation. The principal lesson is how forecast information can be packaged for operational decisions, not a proven social benefit in Kazakhstan. [18]

What transfers: actionable operational interfacesWhat does not: vendor claims as causal proof
03 / COMMUNITY DISSEMINATION TRIALINDIA · YALE / BIHAR

Community messengers + AI forecasts

A four-year randomized trial in Bihar covered 319 local government areas with around 3.6 million residents. In a September 2026 brief, researchers report that trained community messengers more than tripled warning receipt and increased the likelihood of warnings arriving before floodwater by roughly 30%. This is evidence for the community dissemination layer in that setting—not proof that AI alone produced the gains. [20]

What transfers: test human channels alongside modelsWhat does not: assuming identical effect sizes in Kazakhstan

✳ CRITICAL COST ASSESSMENT 05 DIMENSIONS

The real price
of prediction.

Implementation costs are more than software licenses. Below are five questions a real pilot must answer, along with the minimum evidence needed for a defensible budget. We do not present speculative prices as verified Kazakhstan procurement costs.

↯

Environmental

Processing, hosting and storage consume electricity. Compare a modest local or cloud model against reusing existing institutional infrastructure; account for model updates, hardware, grid emissions and cooling where measurable.

REQUIRES: kWh logs × electricity emission factor
₸

Economic

Budget for data integration, design, validation, staff training, multilingual quality assurance, communications and ongoing maintenance. Include the costs borne by municipalities and community partners—not just vendor fees.

REQUIRES: vendor quotes + staff time + channel prices
♙

Labor

Forecast review, hotline work, translation, volunteer coordination and emergency outreach require trained people. Automation might shift work toward quality assurance rather than remove it, and additional unpaid work must not be hidden.

REQUIRES: staff-hours, pay rates and roles
⌗

Governance

Emergency authorities must retain final control. Rules should specify who can initiate outreach, who checks a model's reasoning, how disputes are handled, and who is accountable for an incorrect alert or missed neighborhood.

REQUIRES: agency agreement, audit trail, legal review
◌

Data ethics

Aggregated neighborhood indicators are preferable to unneeded personal profiles. Test whether false negatives cluster in poorly monitored places, minimize retention, protect location data and provide a path to correct errors.

REQUIRES: impact assessment + subgroup error audit
Budget formula to validate: Total pilot cost = setup + equipment + data integration + paid labor + messaging/outreach + maintenance + independent evaluation. Cost-effectiveness should be measured as cost per additional household receiving a timely, understood warning versus a non-AI alternative, not simply messages sent.
THE DECISION RULE

When would the cost be justified?

AI should be adopted only if an independent pilot demonstrates measurable improvements over a strengthened human-led warning system. Evaluation must include timely warning receipt, operational costs, staff workload and unequal coverage. If conventional outreach achieves comparable outcomes at lower cost, Kazakhstan should prioritize the non-AI alternative. Reliable financial estimates require actual procurement quotations and operating data.

OUR PROVISIONAL POLICY VERDICT

Start small.
Prove the last mile.

Conditional pilot only. Kazakhstan should not buy a second generic forecasting platform to address a communication problem. A limited, independently evaluated Petropavl pilot could be justified if it builds on official forecasts, protects privacy, includes non-digital channels, and demonstrates an improvement over strengthening human-led outreach alone.

The decisive evidence is not how accurately the tool draws a flood map. It is whether more households receive warnings early enough to understand and act, at an acceptable cost and without systematically excluding particular communities.

What a real pilot must measure

Baseline information should be collected at community level through voluntary surveys and careful administrative records: warning receipt, delivery channel, lead time, comprehension, intended action, feasible evacuation support, false alarms, and differences across areas. Any study design must preserve standard emergency protection for all participants. The central test is whether AI-assisted prioritization improves outcomes beyond a well-funded, human-led multichannel warning program.

A further limitation concerns evidence transfer. The cited research draws on different countries, hydrological settings, governance arrangements, and populations. Those cases establish mechanisms and feasible technologies, not a guaranteed effect size for Petropavl.
